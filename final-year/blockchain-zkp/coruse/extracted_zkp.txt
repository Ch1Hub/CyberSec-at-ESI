Motivation

--- SLIDE 4 ---
Traditional verification :
- The verifier asks to see the underlying 
data or recompute the full operation. 
-  Sensitive evidence is copied, stored, 
and potentially leaked. 
- Trust is placed in the verifier’s handling 
of the data. 
What problem does a zero-knowledge proof solve? 
Zero-knowledge verification:
- The prover sends a proof derived from 
the hidden evidence. 
- The verifier checks the claim without 
receiving the witness. 
- Correctness is enforced 
cryptographically rather than by data 
disclosure. 
Prove the Claim not the data

--- SLIDE 5 ---
Prove “I am at least 18” without revealing the birth date 
- Public statement: the user satisfies the age policy for a 
specific service and date.
- Private witness: the birth date, credential signature, and any 
secret authentication data. 
- The proof should reveal only “policy satisfied,” not the exact 
date of birth. 
- The public inputs must bind the proof to the intended context, 
such as the service, policy version, and session. 
Statement = what may be public 
Witness = the secret evidence

Foundations

--- SLIDE 7 ---
Statements, witnesses, and the relation R(x, w) 
Public statement x 
- The claim that the verifier is allowed to 
know. 
- Examples: a hash, a Merkle root, a 
threshold, a public output, a policy 
identifier. 
Private witness w 
- The secret data that makes the 
statement true. 
- Examples: a password, credential, 
private key, path in a Merkle tree, or 
computation trace. 
The prover convinces the verifier that  ∃ w such that R(x, w) = 1. 

--- SLIDE 8 ---
The three guarantees of a zero-knowledge proof 
Completeness :  If the statement is true and both parties follow the protocol, the verifier 
accepts. 
Soundness :  A prover without a valid witness cannot make the verifier accept, except with 
negligible probability. 
Zero knowledge : The verifier learns nothing beyond the fact that the statement is true.
Privacy without soundness is useless; soundness without privacy is not zero knowledge.

Types of ZKP

--- SLIDE 10 ---
Interactive zero knowledge: challenge and response 
- The prover enters one branch of the cave while the 
verifier waits outside. 
- The verifier randomly asks the prover to return through 
branch A or branch B. 
- A prover who knows the secret door password can 
always comply. 
- A prover who guessed the initial branch succeeds only 
when the challenge matches the guess. 
One round convinces only probabilistically. 
A B

--- SLIDE 11 ---
Why repetition makes cheating unlikely 
- Each fresh challenge removes another factor of uncertainty.  
- The argument assumes challenges are unpredictable and independent. 
- Cryptographic protocols choose parameters so the remaining error is negligible. 


--- SLIDE 12 ---
The generic three-move Σ-protocol pattern 
- The verifier checks an algebraic relation involving public statement,commitment, 
challenge, and response. 
- The response proves consistency with a witness without revealing the witness. 
- Schnorr Protocol is the classic example of this pattern

--- SLIDE 14 ---
Non-interactive zero knowledge: one proof, many verifiers 
- The prover publishes a single proof instead of participating in a live conversation. 
- Any verifier with the public parameters and statement can check the proof later. 
- This model fits blockchains, APIs, documents, and asynchronous systems.

The high-level pipeline 
- The computation is rewritten as algebra over a finite field. 
- The witness contains private inputs and every intermediate wire value. 
- The proof compresses satisfaction of many constraints into a few group 
elements.

--- SLIDE 20 ---
Step 1: express the computation as an arithmetic circuit 
- Circuit operations are additions and multiplications in a finite field Fₚ
- Every wire carries a field element; comparisons and bit operations require 
explicit constraints. 
- The witness records the value of every wire: inputs, outputs, and intermediate 
results. 
- The circuit checks equations; it does not “run ordinary code” in the usual CPU 
sense. 


--- SLIDE 21 ---
Step 2 R1CS: one multiplication of linear combinations 
- z is the witness vector containing 1, public inputs, private inputs, and 
intermediate wires. 
- Each row i selects three linear combinations of z. 
- One row enforces one rank-1 constraint: left × right = output. 
- Addition is absorbed inside the linear combinations; multiplication creates the 
constraint boundary. 


--- SLIDE 22 ---
R1CS worked example for y = x³ + x + 5 
- For x = 3 and y = 35, the witness is [1, 35, 3, 9, 27]. 
- A wrong intermediate value breaks at least one constraint. 
- The final proof attests that all rows are satisfied simultaneously. 


--- SLIDE 23 ---
Step 3 : compress all constraints into one polynomial identity 
- Each R1CS row becomes an evaluation point of interpolation polynomials. 
- T(X) vanishes at every constraint point. 
- If all constraints are satisfied, U(X)V(X) − W(X) is divisible by T(X). 
- The quotient H(X) exists only when the complete witness satisfies the system. 


--- SLIDE 24 ---
Step 4 : evaluate at a hidden point τ 
- Setup samples secret values such as τ and publishes structured group 
encodings. 
- The prover combines those encodings according to the witness and circuit 
polynomials. 
- Because τ stays hidden, the prover cannot freely invent arbitrary evaluations. 
- The verifier checks encoded algebraic relations rather than receiving the 
witness.

Applications

--- SLIDE 26 ---
zk-rollups 
- A rollup executes many transactions outside the base 
chain. 
- The prover generates a validity proof for the resulting 
state transition. 
- An on-chain verifier accepts the batch only when the 
proof is valid. 
- Succinct verification lets one proof represent a large 
amount of computation. 


--- SLIDE 27 ---
Private Transactions
- Represent hidden notes with public commitments that bind their contents. 
- Prove membership and the required spending authorisation without revealing the chosen note.
- Prove that bounded input values equal bounded output values plus the fee. 
- Publish correctly derived nullifiers; the ledger rejects repeated nullifiers to prevent double-spending.

--- SLIDE 32 ---
What to remember 
- A ZKP establishes a public statement without exposing extra information about its witness. 
- Interactive and non-interactive protocols differ in how prover and verifier communicate. 
- zk-SNARKs add succinctness and an argument-of-knowledge guarantee. 
- Circuits and constraints define the claim; the proof system supplies cryptographic 
enforcement. 
- Privacy, data authenticity, replay protection and application correctness require deliberate 
design.
